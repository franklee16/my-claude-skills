> **3.** *Here is what bothers me most. The paper's story is that retail attention drives mispricing. But retail investors cannot move prices in stocks dominated by institutions. If your mechanism were real, the predictability should disappear in the high-institutional-ownership subsample — and unless I am misreading your Table 5, that is exactly what happens: the long-short spread is insignificant there. Doesn't this contradict your story? Relatedly, even in the full sample the spread of 31 bps per month is tiny — the annual turnover of this strategy would not cover its transaction costs. Please answer directly, and justify the economic magnitude of your findings.*

Thank you very much for pointing out this very important issue. <!-- SRC: style §5.1 -->
We agree with you that retail investors are less likely to move prices in stocks dominated by institutions, and that this heterogeneity is central to the mechanism we propose. <!-- SRC: style §5.1 -->

To answer your question directly: you are reading Table 5 correctly. The long-short spread is statistically indistinguishable from zero in the high-institutional-ownership subsample, and this is the pattern our mechanism predicts. If retail attention drives mispricing, the predictability should concentrate in stocks that retail investors dominate, which is the logic of your comment. In the previous draft we left this heterogeneity implicit in Table 5; your comment identified this gap in our presentation, and the revised paper now states the heterogeneity as a prediction of the mechanism and tests it formally.

Following your comment, <!-- SRC: style §5.2 --> we now formally test this heterogeneity by interacting ATTN with a high-institutional-ownership indicator (the top tercile of institutional ownership, constructed from Thomson Reuters 13F holdings). <!-- SRC: style §6.7 (data-provenance pattern) --> The results are reported in Table IA.2 of the Internet Appendix and discussed in a new paragraph at the end of Section 4.3 (page 21) of the revised manuscript. <!-- SRC: style §8.5 --> In the full sample, the baseline ATTN coefficient is 0.032 (t-stat. = 3.05). <!-- SRC: style §7.3 --> The coefficient on the ATTN × High-IO interaction is −0.011 (t-stat. = −1.05): it enters with the negative sign the mechanism predicts, though it is individually imprecise. In the subsample splits, the long-short spread is 0.44% per month (t-stat. = 2.62) among low-IO stocks, and 0.09% per month (t-stat. = 0.71), statistically indistinguishable from zero, among high-IO stocks. <!-- SRC: strategy §5.2 (disclose unfavorable sub-results) --> This seems to suggest that the predictability of ATTN is concentrated in stocks that retail investors dominate, in line with your conjecture. <!-- SRC: style §6.7; §5.7 -->

On the economic magnitude, we acknowledge that the full-sample spread of 31 basis points per month is modest in absolute terms. <!-- SRC: strategy §4.6 step 2 --> We justify the magnitude on two grounds. First, the full-sample estimate averages across subsamples in which the mechanism operates at very different intensities: 0.44% per month where retail investors dominate the ownership structure, and 0.09% where institutions do. The economically relevant magnitude for the mechanism is therefore the low-IO spread, roughly 1.4 times the full-sample figure. Second and more importantly, <!-- SRC: style §9.5 --> relative to the typical monthly price movement in our sample (the mean absolute deviation of monthly returns is 6.8%), the low-IO spread of 0.44% represents about 6.5% of a typical month's move. <!-- SRC: strategy §4.6 step 3 (distributional benchmark); style §7.4 --> Overall, we interpret these magnitudes as evidence on the size of attention-driven mispricing, which is the object our analysis is designed to measure. <!-- SRC: style §9.6 -->

We also agree that implementability net of transaction costs is an important consideration for judging economic magnitude. We would be pleased to add an analysis of net-of-cost returns to the paper if you prefer. <!-- SRC: style §6.9 -->
